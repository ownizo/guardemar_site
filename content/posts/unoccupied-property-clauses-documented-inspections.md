Many homes in Portugal are occupied seasonally. An owner may spend several weeks in the Algarve, return to another country and leave the property closed until the next visit. That pattern is normal for a second home, but it may also be relevant to the wording of the property’s insurance policy.

Home insurance policies commonly distinguish between an occupied home and one that has been unoccupied for a defined period. The exact definition, period and consequence are matters for the individual policy. They can vary between insurers, products and the circumstances of a claim.

This makes it important to read the policy rather than rely on a general assumption about what “unoccupied” means.

## Start with the policy wording

An unoccupied-property clause may set out conditions that apply when nobody is living at the home for a stated period. Depending on the contract, those conditions may concern water systems, heating, security, regular visits or notification to the insurer.

There is no responsible universal summary of those requirements. One policy may define occupancy differently from another. A main residence, holiday home and property intended for letting may also be treated differently.

Owners should therefore ask their insurer or insurance adviser clear questions:

- How does this policy define an unoccupied property?
- Does the policy require the insurer to be told about extended absence?
- Are regular visits required, and if so, what should a visit include?
- Are there conditions relating to water, electricity, alarms or locks?
- What records should be retained?
- Does the cover change during an extended vacant period?

The answers should come from the insurer, intermediary or policy documents. A property-care company cannot amend the insurance contract or guarantee how a future claim will be assessed.

## Why a visit record is useful

A casual message saying that a house “looked fine” provides little detail. A structured inspection record can establish a more useful chronology.

A dated report can show when somebody entered the property, which agreed areas were visually checked, what was observed and whether photographs were taken. If a concern was found, the report can also show when the owner was informed and what next action was recommended.

That record may help an owner explain the practical oversight in place during an absence. It can also help a contractor understand when a visible change was first identified.

The value lies in consistency. Repeated reports make it easier to compare the property’s normal condition with a later observation. A new ceiling mark, changed pool level, unusual moisture beneath a sink or damaged shutter has more context when previous reports are available.

## Photographs add context, not certainty

Photographs can support a written observation. They may record the visible condition of a room, access point, water area, terrace, pool or garden on the date of a visit.

They do not prove that every concealed system was sound. A photograph cannot confirm the condition of hidden pipework, wiring inside a wall, a roof area that was not safely accessible or a technical component that requires specialist testing.

For that reason, a home watch report should describe its scope accurately. It is a visual, non-invasive record. It is not a building survey, engineering opinion, electrical inspection, plumbing test or certificate of compliance.

## Detection time matters

Many property losses become more serious because time passes before anybody notices them. A small leak can affect cabinetry or flooring. Wind can displace an exterior item. A failed irrigation component can leave a garden dry or allow water to run unnecessarily. Humidity indicators can develop in a closed room.

Scheduled visits reduce the interval between an observable change and the owner becoming aware of it. They do not prevent every incident, but they create a recurring opportunity to identify visible concerns and decide what should happen next.

The appropriate frequency depends on the property, season, systems, location, insurer requirements and owner instructions. A villa with a pool, irrigation and several exterior access points has a different profile from an apartment in a managed building.

## Agree what happens after a problem is found

An inspection routine is more useful when the response process is decided in advance. The property record should identify how the owner can be contacted, who may authorise a contractor, whether water may be isolated where safe and what spending approval applies.

If damage is discovered, the owner should also follow the notification and loss-mitigation requirements in the policy. That may include contacting the insurer or adviser promptly and preserving relevant evidence. The correct steps depend on the contract and circumstances.

## Keep records in an organised form

Useful records may include inspection reports, dated photographs, owner instructions, contractor attendance details, invoices and communications concerning an identified issue. Documents should be retained securely and shared only with people who have a proper reason to receive them.

The record should remain factual. It should distinguish between what was observed, what the owner reported and what a qualified contractor diagnosed. Avoiding speculation makes the chronology clearer.

## What documented inspections cannot promise

Documented home watch visits do not guarantee insurance cover, claim acceptance or detection of every defect. They do not replace policy compliance, maintenance, security measures or specialist inspections. They also do not provide insurance advice.

Their role is narrower and practical: to provide scheduled physical presence, a dated visual record and a clear route for reporting and coordinating action.

For an overseas owner, that can be an important part of responsible property oversight. The starting point remains the same: understand the policy, agree the inspection scope and keep evidence that accurately reflects what happened.
