Many of the homes we look after hold more than furniture. A painting bought on the day the house was finished, a cellar built up over years, books, textiles, a piano. While the owner is in residence, these things are noticed every day. When the house is closed for months, nobody is looking at them at all.

The Algarve is kind to people and less kind to some objects. Salt air, strong sun through large windows, closed rooms and the shift from a dry summer to a damp winter all leave their mark on a house that nobody opens.

## What changes when the house is closed

The risks are rarely dramatic. They are slow:

- **Humidity.** A closed house with the shutters down can hold damp air for weeks, particularly after autumn rain. Canvas, paper, wood and leather respond to that long before a stain appears on a wall.
- **Light.** Sun moves across a room through the year. A work that hung in shade in spring may sit in direct afternoon light in winter.
- **Temperature.** A wine store without active cooling follows the house. A power cut or a tripped breaker can stop a cellar unit without anyone knowing.
- **Pests.** Moths and wood-boring insects favour quiet rooms. Their early signs are small and easy to miss.
- **Water.** A small leak above a wall where art hangs is a different problem from a leak in a garage.

None of this needs a crisis to cause a loss. It needs time and no one watching.

## What a documented visit can and cannot do

On a Guardemar visit we record what we see, in the same places each time: the condition of rooms, signs of damp or condensation, the humidity reading, whether the cellar unit is running and showing its usual display, and anything that has changed since the last visit. The photographs are taken from the same viewpoints, so a slow change becomes visible over a season.

We do not move, clean, handle or value artworks or collections, and we do not give conservation or insurance advice. Where something needs a specialist — a conservator, a cellar engineer, an electrician — we tell you, with the photographs, and arrange access once you have approved the work.

## Practical steps before you leave

A short list, agreed in advance, works better than a long one:

1. **Note what matters most.** A simple room-by-room list of the pieces you want checked on every visit, with a reference photograph of each.
2. **Ask a specialist about placement.** A conservator can advise on walls, light and humidity for particular works far better than a general rule can.
3. **Check the cellar's power.** Know which breaker serves the cooling unit, and ask whether it restarts by itself after a cut.
4. **Agree what we may do.** Whether we may open shutters to ventilate, close curtains in a south-facing room, or switch on a dehumidifier, should be written into your instructions.
5. **Speak to your insurer.** Valuable items often need to be scheduled separately, and some policies have conditions for long absences. [Questions to ask your insurer](/blog/questions-to-ask-insurer-second-home-portugal/) is a useful starting point.

## Keep the record together

The visit reports, the reference photographs and any specialist's notes belong in the same place. If a piece is ever damaged, a dated record of its condition and of the room it hung in is far more useful than a recollection. [A simple evidence file](/blog/evidence-file-vacant-algarve-home/) explains how to keep it factual.

If your home holds a collection that should be checked on every visit, mention it when you [request a private assessment](/contact/). Estates with several buildings or a cellar are usually better served by a [Private Estate proposal](/plans/) than by a standard plan.
