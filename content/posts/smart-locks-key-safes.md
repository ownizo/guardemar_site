For an owner abroad, keyless access is appealing. No keys to post, no neighbour to call, no lost spare. A code can be sent to the cleaner on Monday and changed on Tuesday. But keyless systems have their own risks, and they do not remove the need for someone to manage access.

## Key safes

An external key safe with a combination is cheap and simple. It is also the least secure option. Codes are shared and rarely changed, safes can be forced, and a key safe on the wall tells everyone that a key is close by. Some insurers may take a view on how keys are stored, so check your policy.

If you use one, choose a sturdy model, fix it properly, keep it out of sight from the street and change the code regularly.

## Smart locks

Smart locks allow access by app, code or card, often with a log of who entered and when. Advantages include individual codes for each person, time-limited access and remote changes.

Practical issues to consider:

- **Batteries.** Most smart locks run on batteries. A flat battery at the wrong moment can lock everyone out.
- **Connectivity.** Some features depend on Wi-Fi or a hub. A power cut or router failure can disable remote control.
- **Backup access.** Many smart locks still need a physical key as a backup. That key needs managing too.
- **Salt and weather.** Exterior locks near the sea need to withstand corrosion.
- **Condominium doors.** In apartments, the building entrance may still need a physical key or fob.

## Codes are only as good as their management

The real security question is not the lock, but how codes are issued and withdrawn. Ask:

- who has a code at this moment?
- when was each code last changed?
- are codes for cleaners and contractors removed when they no longer need them?
- is there a record of who entered, and is anyone actually looking at it?
